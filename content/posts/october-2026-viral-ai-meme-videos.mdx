## 1. The Closed on Sunday Choir

**What it is.** You stand calm and silent in the dead center while a huge robed crowd sings, sways and raises its hands on the hillside behind you. The composition copies the final scene of Kanye West's 2019 "Closed on Sunday" music video, where the Sunday Service Choir surrounds him.

**Where it came from.** The AI versions appeared on TikTok around October 14, 2026. The one that actually kicked it off was a "cat choir" version — the lead and the entire choir behind are fluffy cats — and that contrast pushed the format into a full trend. To see versions spreading now, search the TikTok tag [#closedonsunday](https://www.tiktok.com/tag/closedonsunday).

**Why it works.** A still center against a moving crowd is instantly recognizable, and the lead can be a person, a pet, even skeleton angels — the room to remix is almost endless.

## 2. The Jean Phil Dance (Toc Toc / Kicékéla)

**What it is.** A "French gentleman" with a blond bob, a handlebar moustache and a brown houndstooth suit hops on one foot, deadpan, and knocks on a door that isn't there. The moves have energy; the dancer has none — and that contrast is the whole joke.

**Where it came from.** Per [Know Your Meme](https://www.knowyourmeme.com/memes/jean-philanthrope-jean-phil), Jean Phil (full name Jean Philanthrope) is himself an AI character: the earliest post appeared on Instagram on September 17, 2026, TikTok followed two days later, and the account passed 145,000 followers in three days. The soundtrack is Patrick Sébastien's 2015 song "Toc toc" (French for "knock knock"). Because he was generated to begin with, people felt free to swap themselves, their friends and even their dogs into his spot. To see versions, search the TikTok tag [#jeanphil](https://www.tiktok.com/tag/jeanphil).

**Why it works.** A character you recognize in half a second, one fixed routine, and only the lead changes per clip — that is the exact template structure built to be remade endlessly.

## 3. The Gangnam Style Trio

**What it is.** Three friends take turns at one phone camera singing the chorus while the others dance behind, then join for a group finale. PSY's old hit found a new format and came roaring back.

**Where it came from.** PSY's 2012 smash returned in October 2026: @emiliyyam's night shoot in Seoul's Gangnam district on October 5 hit 17.5 million views and led the wave; from October 12 the AI versions took off, recasting all three leads with people from photos. To see versions, search the TikTok tag [#gangnamstyle](https://www.tiktok.com/tag/gangnamstyle).

**Why it works.** More people on screen means twice the faces in the thumbnail and twice the friends tagging each other to share — it is built for spreading across friend groups.

## What Do These Meme Videos Have in Common?

Watch enough of them and you notice: what actually spreads is never a single video, but a **repeatable format**:

1. **A scene you recognize instantly** — a choir, a white room, a banquet hall, Gangnam at night. The backdrop is fixed, so viewers get the joke with zero effort.
2. **The lead is the only variable** — scene and moves stay the same; each clip only swaps the person in the middle. So anyone can drop themselves in.
3. **Contrast makes the joke** — a quiet center against a frantic crowd, energetic steps on a blank face, cats infiltrating a solemn choir.
4. **Vertical, short, loopable** — 9:16, around ten seconds, watched in one pass and shared in the next.
5. **The original sound is the entry ticket** — adding the trend audio inside the app is how the algorithm files your clip under the meme.

For creators the lesson is to learn the format, not chase one viral clip — trends rotate within weeks, but "recognize the scene, swap the lead" keeps working.
