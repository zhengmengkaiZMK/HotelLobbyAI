## What the clip actually does with your photos

Knowing the routine helps you pick the photos. The template runs one static shot, about eighteen seconds, of two dancers side by side in a living room:

1. They groove side to side on the funk remix, shoulders loose, knees bent.
2. A one-hand wave toward the camera — a little salute to the town heading out to party.
3. They turn around together and keep dancing facing away from the camera.
4. They spin back to face the lens, right on the build-up.
5. On the final "huss, huss, huss" beat, both run in place pumping their fists like sprinters.

Your photos only control two of those beats: who is dancing. The room, the choreography, the timing and the sound are all generated from the template, which is why two photos are the entire production budget.

## The photos that survive the render

Two inputs means twice the ways to get it wrong, so here is where the failures actually come from:

- **Two separate photos, never one group shot.** Each dancer gets their own upload slot. A single photo with both of you in it confuses the casting — the model blends or picks one face, and both results look wrong.
- **Faces clear and well lit, both of you.** No sunglasses, no heavy filters. The turn-around move swings the heads fast, and a soft face drifts exactly when it is most visible.
- **Full body or knee-up.** This one matters more than in most trends: the routine shows your back. If the photo is cropped at the chest, the model has to invent what you look like from behind, and invented outfits look invented.
- **Match the two photos to each other.** Same kind of lighting, same kind of framing. A headshot next to a full-body shot makes the pair dance on different timing, and the sync — the whole charm of the format — falls apart.
- **One adult per photo, permission confirmed.** Photos of minors, celebrities, or anyone who did not agree get rejected by moderation, and those are the posts that get accounts in trouble.

## The mistakes that actually break renders

- **Mismatched photo styles.** One daylight photo plus one heavily filtered selfie makes the pair look like they were edited from two different memes. Keep the two inputs consistent.
- **Head-only crops.** The back-turn move needs something to show. Chest-up photos give the model nothing for the second half of the routine.
- **Three-person wishful thinking.** The format holds exactly two dancers. If you upload three photos hoping for a K-pop group version, the third one gets ignored — the group versions on TikTok are a different format entirely.
- **Expecting the Roblox character.** The original was a stiff basketball avatar in a game engine. This template recreates the two-dancer dance that the audio spawned on 17 November — real people, real living room, same sound and same routine.
