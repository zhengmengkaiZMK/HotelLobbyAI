## Where the trend started

The earliest posts we could verify landed on TikTok on 20 November 2026, from two accounts: @po94275 and @lunita35787. Both used the same image-to-video effect — a woman on a seaside promenade lifted by two enormous golden hands — and the caption that became the format's signature: "God literally picked me up. This AI effect looks SO real... Wait for the hand of God."

The @lunita35787 version is the one most people saw. It passed 100,000 views within five days, riding a sound listed simply as "sonido original". Nothing about the launch looked special — no celebrity, no challenge, no brand. It was one effect, one caption, and a visual so simple it explains itself in the first second.

## The timeline: one week, global

- **20 November 2026** — the first verified posts go up on TikTok. Two accounts, same effect, same caption formula.
- **21 November 2026** — the trend takes off. Spanish-language tutorials appear the same day, naming it "La Mano de Dios" and the "Heaven Bound" trend, and the effect starts crossing between the English-speaking and Spanish-speaking feeds at full speed.
- **22–23 November 2026** — roughly 30 posts from about 20 accounts carry the same caption across the US and Mexico, totaling around 250,000 plays. Pet versions and couple versions show up.
- **24–26 November 2026** — AI video template sites race to ship one-photo generators of the format. The original passes 100,000 views, and US search interest for "Hand of God" climbs to near parity with "GPTs" on Google Trends — rare air for a meme that is barely a week old.

That last line is the one worth reading twice. Search interest at "GPTs" level means people are actively looking for how to make the video, not just watching it — the moment a meme converts viewers into makers is the moment its second wave starts.

## Why it spread this fast

**The image is universal.** Being lifted into the light reads as blessing, victory, or absolution — and it reads that way in every language and every culture at once. Most memes need the joke explained; this one does not even need a caption, which is why the Spanish-language half of the trend grew exactly as fast as the English half.

**The entry bar is one photo.** No dance to learn, no timing to hit, no friend to recruit, no outfit to plan. Every viewer is one upload away from their own version, and template sites turned that upload into a single tap during the trend's first week. Compare that with dance trends, where the cost of entry is learning choreography in your bedroom with the curtains open.

**The result is emotionally quotable.** The flash-to-white moment and the rise above the coast form a clean before-and-after that still works when it loops for the tenth time. And the caption formula — "wait for the hand of God" — turns every repost into a setup and a punchline. The comments fill with who should get lifted next, which is free engagement the algorithm reads as signal.

**The name carried it further than it deserved to go.** "Hand of God" is also the most famous phrase in football history, and the collision generated a small storm of confused curiosity searches. None of those searches find a handball — they find the golden hands — and some of them stay. (If you came here for Maradona: wrong trend. The only thing being lifted here is a person, by hands made of light.)

## Is it AI? Is anyone actually being lifted?

It is AI end to end. The original posts are image-to-video generations — nobody was lifted, the promenade is generated, and the people in the clips are AI-made. The honest posts carry the AI-generated label that TikTok and Instagram both offer, and the responsible ones use photos of themselves or adults who agreed to be in the video.

That matters more than usual with this trend, because the imagery borrows religious language. The format works best when it stays a joke — "God literally picked me up" is a caption, not a testimony. Keep the playfulness and the format keeps its charm.
