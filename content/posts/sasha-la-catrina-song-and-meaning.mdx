Every video in the **Sasha La Catrina** trend is stamped by one sound — a soft Spanish farewell sung over the transformation: *"Adiós, señora bonita."* The line is short, warm and a little heartbreaking, and it is the reason a five-second AI reveal reads as a whole story. This page unpacks the line, the film it comes from, the character behind the trend's name, and the holiday that carries all of it.

If you arrived here without the context, start with [what is Sasha La Catrina](/blog/what-is-sasha-la-catrina) — the trend itself and its timeline are covered there.

## What "Adiós señora bonita" means

In Spanish it means **"Goodbye, pretty lady."** Spoken to the Catrina as the transformation completes, it works as both a farewell and a compliment: the ordinary person in the photo is gone, and in her place stands the most elegant skeleton in Mexico. The line's affectionate tone is why it spread — it is a goodbye that sounds like a blessing.

In the trend's caption culture the line doubled: *"adiós Sasha, adiós señora bonita"* says goodbye to "Sasha" — the nickname the trend gave its star — while the sound says goodbye to the "before" version of you. One phrase, two exits.

## Where the line comes from — The Book of Life

The sound traces to ***The Book of Life*** (2014), the animated celebration of Mexican folk culture produced by Guillermo del Toro and directed by Jorge Gutiérrez. The film is steeped in Día de Muertos imagery — marigold bridges between the living world and the Land of the Remembered, sugar skulls, candlelit altars — and its Spanish-language lines became the trend's audio bed.

That matters for the trend's look, too. The fairy-tale Catrina in every viral clip — the white sugar-skull makeup with elegant detail, the embroidered lace gown with marigolds woven in, the orange-and-purple flower crown — borrows the film's folk-art palette directly. The trend is not transforming you into a random skeleton; it is transforming you into *that movie's* version of the character, with that movie's voice saying goodbye.

## Why the trend calls her Sasha

"Sasha" is not the character's name in any film or tradition. The biggest video of the wave — Đan Thy's flower-toss reveal from 20 October 2026, past 40.6M plays — ran the Spanish caption *"adiós SHASHA, adiós señora bonita"*, and the CapCut template that spread days later took the name **Sasha La Catrina** with it. It is an affectionate nickname for **La Catrina** herself, the way the internet usually adopts a character: a pet name, a sound, and a format lock in together.

## Who La Catrina actually is

The character carries more than a century of history, which is part of why the trend feels bigger than a meme:

- **c. 1910** — printmaker **José Guadalupe Posada** etches a skeleton in a fancy European hat, *la calavera garbancera*, satirizing Mexicans who disowned their roots to mimic aristocrats.
- **1947** — muralist **Diego Rivera** paints her into *Dream of a Sunday Afternoon in Alameda Central Park*, names her **La Catrina**, and turns a satire into a national icon.
- **Today** — she is the face of **Día de Muertos** (1–2 November), the holiday when families welcome the souls of loved ones back with ofrendas: marigolds, candles, sugar skulls, photographs. Every element the AI trend borrows — the cempasúchil petals, the candles, the lace, the skull face — is an ofrenda element first.

The farewell in the sound fits the holiday perfectly. Día de Muertos is not a mourning holiday; it is a welcome-back holiday, and every welcome implies a goodbye. *"Adiós, señora bonita"* is the whole holiday compressed into three words.

## Why the sound makes the trend

1. **Instant recognition.** The feed is flooded with Catrina reveals; the spoken line files every one of them under the same meme before the transformation even finishes.

3. **A loop with a plot.** Ordinary person → goodbye → Catrina. Three beats, five seconds, replay-ready.
